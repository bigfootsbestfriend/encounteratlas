If something is watching you

Dogman reports have a pattern: a dark road or a deer stand, a growl or a smell, then eyes at a height that doesn't make sense. Whatever is out there, the safe response is the same, and it's the same response that gets you the best record.

  1. Stay put and stay calm. Don't run. Running from any predator, from a coyote to a cougar, can trigger a chase.
  2. Keep your light on it and get bigger. Stand up straight, face it, hold your arms or jacket out. If you're with others, group up shoulder to shoulder.
  3. Hit record. Phone voice recorder first (it works in total darkness), then video if you can hold the light on it. Say the time out loud.
  4. Make noise. Talk loudly and firmly. Don't scream, which can sound like prey.
  5. Back away slowly toward your vehicle, camp or the trail, keeping it in view. Don't turn your back and don't follow it if it leaves.
  6. If it attacks, fight back. For nearly every animal you could meet at night in North America, fighting back hard is the right call. The exception is a defensive grizzly attack, where lying flat and protecting your neck is advised.
  7. Once you're safe, write it down. Before you talk it over, before you sleep, before you read anyone else's story.

Never

  • Never shoot at eyes or a shape you can't fully identify. Hunters and hikers wear headlamps; eyeshine from a dog looks like eyeshine from a coyote. This is how people get killed.
  • Never follow it into the woods in the dark. Come back in daylight, with someone, and look for tracks then.
  • Never let pets off the lead at night in the backcountry. A dog that chases something into the dark is the start of a lot of bad stories.

Eyeshine: who's looking back at you?

Many animals have a mirror-like layer at the back of the eye (the tapetum lucidum) that bounces light back out, which is why their eyes glow in your beam. The colour, the height, the spacing and the way the eyes move all help narrow it down.

The puzzle

Humans, chimps and gorillas don't have eyeshine, which is why a person standing in your beam doesn't glow. Yet glowing eyes are one of the most common details in Bigfoot and Dogman reports. Some researchers argue an unknown animal could simply have the reflective layer; sceptics say the glowing eyes usually belong to a known animal at a confusing height. Either way, eye colour and height are worth recording carefully.

Why the height can fool you

  • Slopes: a deer on a bank above a road can put its eyes 7 or 8 feet above the road surface.
  • Logs, rocks and stumps: a coyote standing on a fallen tree is suddenly a tall coyote.
  • Trees: raccoons, bobcats, house cats and owls all climb or perch.
  • Distance: in the dark you can't judge range well, so you can't judge height either. Note what's around the eyes (fence posts, a car, a tree) and measure it in daylight.
  • Spacing: eyes far apart mean a big head close by, or something very big further off. Close-set eyes at head height may be a raccoon in a tree, not a giant.

When you hear something, record it, then estimate the direction with your phone compass and how far away it seemed. Note the time and whether anything else (dogs, coyotes, birds) reacted. Then run it past the Merlin app and the library before calling it unknown.

Documenting a night encounter

Audio first

Sound works in the dark and your phone can always do it. Start recording as soon as anything happens and narrate: time, what you see, which way.

Then video

Phone "night mode" stills smear anything that moves. Video with your torch on the subject is better. Hold still and don't zoom in digitally.

Thermal, if you have it

A thermal monocular or camera shows warm bodies through brush and in complete darkness, and many can record. It can't see through glass, so step out of the vehicle or roll the window down.

Mark your spot

Drop a GPS pin where you stood and take a compass bearing toward what you saw. Snap a photo in that direction, even if it's black.

The next morning

  1. Go back in daylight, with someone else.
  2. Stand where you stood and photograph the scene in the direction you were looking.
  3. Have your friend stand where the eyes were. Photograph them from your spot, then measure the height and distance with a tape or rangefinder.
  4. Look for sign: tracks, flattened grass, hair on fences, scat, broken branches. Photograph with scale before touching.
  5. Note what's there: a fence, a bank, a log or a deer trail can explain a lot.

Night safety basics

  • Never alone, never off-trail in the dark. If you are alone (sitting in a stand, driving a forest road), someone should know exactly where you are and when to worry.
  • Two lights, minimum. A headlamp and a bright handheld, with spare batteries.
  • Know the season. Night-time deer poachers and legal night hunting (for hogs, coyotes and raccoons in some states) mean other people with guns and lights may be around.
  • Vehicles are the biggest danger on forest roads. Park well off the road, wear something reflective, and don't stand in the road with a light.
  • Carry bear spray where bears live, and know how to use it before you need it.
  • Cold sneaks up. Sitting still at night, you lose heat fast. Pack a warm layer more than you think you need.

See and be seen

  • Headlamp with red mode
  • Bright handheld torch
  • Spare batteries for both
  • Reflective vest or blaze orange
  • Thermal or night vision (optional)

Record and stay safe

  • Phone charged + power bank
  • Voice recorder app tested
  • Compass app or real compass
  • Bear spray (bear country)
  • Whistle
  • Someone knows where you are
  • Warm layer and hat
