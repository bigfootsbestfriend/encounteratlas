Choosing a camera

You don't need the most expensive camera. You need one that wakes up fast, doesn't announce itself at night, and survives a winter strapped to a tree.

Most important

Trigger speed

How fast it takes a picture after it senses movement. Under half a second is good. Slow cameras catch empty frames and the backsides of things walking away.

Recovery time

How quickly it's ready for the next shot. Matters when something walks past slowly or a group goes by.

Most important

Flash type

No-glow (940 nm) is invisible to people and probably least noticeable to animals, but night images are darker and shorter range. Low-glow (850 nm) shows a faint red glow and lights further. White flash gives colour night photos but everything sees it.

Detection range

How far away the sensor notices warm moving bodies. Most do 40 to 80 feet in theory and less in thick cover. Match it to the spot.

Cellular or not

Cellular cameras send pictures to your phone, so you visit far less. They need signal and a data plan, and some states restrict them during hunting seasons.

Battery life

Look for cameras that take 8 or 12 AAs, or an external battery or solar panel. Cold drains batteries fast.

Tip

Two decent mid-range cameras beat one expensive one. More spots means more chances, and if one gets stolen or eaten by a bear (it happens), you're not starting from zero.

Placement: where, how high, which way

Put cameras where animals are already forced to go: creek crossings, gaps in fences, saddles between valleys, the edges of clearings and fields, and narrow game trails along ridges or drainages. Spots where several trails meet are gold. Avoid hiking trails that people use; you'll fill your cards with hikers, and many people reasonably don't want to be photographed.

  • Face north if you can, or at least avoid east and west. Low sun straight into the lens washes out pictures and can trigger the camera all day.
  • Clear the zone of tall grass and twigs that move in the wind. Snip, don't clear-cut: a natural-looking spot is less likely to be noticed.
  • Think about the background. A tree or hillside behind the subject gives you something to measure against later. Note what it is so you can come back and measure it.
  • Leave a reference. A post, a marked stick or a known tree in frame at a measured distance lets you estimate the height of anything that walks past. Photograph a person of known height standing in the spot when you set up.

  • Time-lapse mode is worth using on big open areas like meadows and clearcuts, where something could be beyond sensor range. One shot every minute or two during dusk and dawn is plenty.
  • Night video eats batteries. If a camera will be out for weeks, use photos at night and video in daytime, or the other way round, depending on what you're after.
  • Turn off any "beep" or LED test lights once it's set.

Batteries, cards and weather

  • Lithium AAs last far longer than alkalines and keep working in the cold. Rechargeable NiMH cells often report "low battery" early because of their lower voltage. Check what your camera supports.
  • An external battery pack or small solar panel makes months-long deployments practical.
  • Use good-quality SD cards no bigger than your camera supports (often 32 GB), and format them in the camera, not your computer.
  • Swap cards; don't review in the field. Carry a fresh card, swap, and look at the old one at home. Less time at the camera means less scent and disturbance.
  • Silica gel packs in the case help stop fogging in damp climates.

Theft, bears and labelling

  • Lock it. A cable lock through the camera's mounting bracket stops casual theft. A steel security box stops most of the rest, and bears.
  • Go high, go off-trail. Cameras at eye level beside a trail disappear. Above head height and 20 feet off the trail, most people never notice.
  • Label it. Put your name and a phone number or email on the camera. Some public land rules and some states require it for property left out, and it means a ranger can call you rather than confiscate it.
  • Bears chew cameras. They seem to like the smell of plastic and of you. A security box and not handling the camera with food on your hands both help.

Bait, lures and ethics

Putting out food changes what you're studying. You'll mostly get raccoons, bears and deer that have learned a free lunch arrives, and you may be breaking the law.

Problems with food bait

  • Feeding bears is illegal in many states and provinces
  • Deer baiting and feeding is restricted in many states, partly to slow chronic wasting disease
  • Habituates animals to people
  • Draws in animals you didn't want, like skunks and bears

Better options

  • Choose spots where animals already travel
  • Non-food curiosities: a hung mirror, a wind chime, a brightly coloured toy
  • Sound lures played in daytime only, where allowed
  • Patience: long deployments over clever tricks

The rules for trail cameras

Trail cameras used to be a free-for-all. Not anymore. Several western states, including Arizona, Nevada and Utah, have restricted trail cameras in recent years, mostly to stop them being used to locate game for hunting. The details (which land, which months, whether it applies if you're not hunting, and how cellular cameras are treated) differ from state to state and change often.

Check the rules for the land you're on

  • State or provincial wildlife regulations: look for sections on trail cameras, "game cameras", baiting and feeding wildlife.
  • The land manager: national forests, BLM offices and state land agencies may limit how long property can be left unattended. On BLM land it can be as short as a few days unless posted otherwise.
  • National parks generally don't allow you to leave equipment, and some don't allow it without a research permit.
  • Private land: permission first, in writing, and tell the owner where the cameras are.

Deployment, step by step

  1. Prep at home. Fresh batteries, formatted card, date and time set, settings checked, name label on.
  2. Pick the spot in daylight. Read the sign: tracks, droppings, rubs, worn paths.
  3. Mount and aim. Strap it firmly, angle it across the trail, tilt it to cover the zone.
  4. Clear the zone and walk-test. Most cameras have a test mode with a light that shows when it detects you. Walk the path at the distance you expect.
  5. Lock it and log it. Lock on, GPS waypoint saved, a note of the camera ID, height, direction and start date.
  6. Leave and stay away. Two to six weeks is a good first check, or less if it's cellular and you can see the battery level.

Pack

  • Cameras with labels
  • Lithium AAs or charged packs
  • Formatted SD cards + spares
  • Straps, mounts and wedge
  • Cable locks or security boxes + keys
  • Pruners for clearing twigs
  • Measuring tape and a reference stick

At each camera

  • Facing north-ish, quartering the trail
  • Height and tilt noted
  • Zone cleared and walk-tested
  • Date and time correct on screen
  • Camera switched ON (yes, really)
  • Locked
  • GPS waypoint + note of camera ID
  • Photo of the setup, with a person for scale

Reviewing thousands of photos

A few weeks can mean thousands of images, most of them leaves. Some tools help:

  • Timelapse, free software made for camera-trap researchers, lets you tag and sort images quickly.
  • MegaDetector, a free AI model widely used by wildlife biologists, flags images that contain an animal, a person or a vehicle so you can skip the empty ones.
  • Keep the original files untouched in one folder and work on copies. The original file's metadata (time, camera model) matters if you ever want anyone to take a picture seriously.
  • Anything odd? Go back to the spot and take a comparison photo with a person or a measuring pole where the subject stood. It answers more questions than any amount of zooming in.

Got something you can't explain? Report it with the original files, and read how to write a credible report.
